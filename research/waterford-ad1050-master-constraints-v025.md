# Waterford / Veðrafjǫrðr AD1050 — Master Reconstruction Constraints v0.25

## Status

`READY_FOR_FIRST_GEOMETRY_D_CANDIDATE`

This is approval to generate and review a **first controlled visual candidate**, not approval of a final historical reconstruction.

Historical claims and mapped geometry remain separate. A precise excavation site does not make an inferred city boundary precise.

---

## 1. Geography lock

The first candidate must read first as a river-and-marsh settlement.

### Must show

- River Suir along the northern / northeastern side of the town.
- St John's River / marsh as a broad southern / southeastern wetland relationship.
- Triangular promontory logic.
- Older eastern Dundory focus with stronger mid-eleventh-century urban development extending westward.
- Natural hydrology visually distinct from any bank or ditch.

### Do not claim

- an exact AD1050 marsh shoreline;
- a surveyed Suir quay line;
- exact Dundory boundary geometry.

The wetland edge should therefore be irregular, soft and visually plausible rather than outlined as a crisp archaeological polygon.

---

## 2. Street and plot lock

### Peter Street

Use Peter Street as the strongest street-axis control for the target phase.

The archaeological synthesis reports a mid- to late-eleventh-century metalled road, a 16 m excavated stretch and an approximate width of 3.6 m, associated with the earliest mid-eleventh-century houses.

For the image this means:

- a clear east-west street axis is appropriate;
- the exact 3.6 m width is **not** a pixel-scale mandate;
- street frontage should be irregular rather than a continuous facade;
- main houses should generally face the street;
- rear buildings, paths, yards and pits should appear behind the frontage.

### High Street

High Street is described in excavation reporting as one of Waterford's principal late-Viking streets, and an early Viking house was recorded at 19–21 High Street.

Use it as a second generalized east-west urban axis.

### Riverward lanes

Synthesis literature describes several north-south roads / lanes crossing the east-west roads and running down toward the Suir.

Use a small number of irregular riverward lanes.

Do **not** use modern road geometry as a literal AD1050 street plan.

### Olaf Street

Late-eleventh-century archaeology can tentatively indicate a lane here, but it is not secure enough to make Olaf Street an exact AD1050 axis in candidate 1.

---

## 3. Urban extent lock

The AD1050 town should be visibly larger than the AD950 eastern-core model.

### Must communicate

- older eastern focus near Dundory;
- substantial westward expansion;
- dense occupation around the Peter Street / High Street zone;
- occupation thinning toward uncertain margins.

### Must not communicate

- the full later medieval / Anglo-Norman city;
- a crisp later stone-wall perimeter;
- uniform density from east to west.

The outer urban envelope is Geometry D.

---

## 4. Domestic architecture lock

Appropriate visual language:

- timber / post-and-wattle construction;
- Type 1 / three-aisled principal houses generalized at street frontage;
- smaller subsidiary / rear structures;
- earth-toned walls and thatch-like roofing as interpretive presentation language;
- irregular gaps between buildings;
- fenced or otherwise readable plots;
- backlands containing paths, pits, storage and modest work areas.

The reconstruction should not reproduce any source excavation plan footprint-by-footprint.

Exact house counts are not historical claims.

---

## 5. Defence lock

A mid-eleventh-century defended-town concept is compatible with the broader evidence, but the exact AD1050 defensive alignment is unresolved.

### Candidate 1 rule

A generalized landward earth bank / ditch may be shown if needed for city legibility, but it must remain visually schematic.

### Hard prohibition

The Bakehouse Lane ditch and bank with timbers dated around AD1070–1080 must **not** be copied backward as the exact AD1050 defensive circuit.

Do not show:

- Anglo-Norman stone walls;
- later mural towers;
- a continuous water-filled moat;
- a line that visually implies surveyed Geometry B precision.

---

## 6. Waterfront lock

The earliest Viking-period quayside remains insufficiently understood in form, date and scale.

### May show

- small craft on the Suir;
- river-facing activity;
- informal landing places;
- short local timber access structures if visually modest.

### Must not show

- a continuous regular quay;
- monumental stone waterfront works;
- long engineered revetments presented as established AD1050 fact;
- a later medieval harbour frontage.

The river edge should read as active but only partly engineered.

---

## 7. Ecclesiastical context

The High Street excavation report describes St Olaf's as originally a Viking foundation, but the exact AD1050 architectural form has not been established to the standard needed for a dominant landmark.

### Candidate 1 rule

Do not place a large stone church as a visual anchor.

If an ecclesiastical cue is used at all, keep it small, non-monumental and explicitly interpretive pending a separate dating review.

---

## 8. Monument exclusions

Do not show the present / later stone form of Reginald's Tower as an AD1050 monument.

Do not import later medieval towers, walls or quay architecture simply because they occupy the same modern area.

---

## 9. Composition / camera

Use the same visual family as the approved Dyflin / Dublin series:

- high-oblique bird's-eye view;
- geography first;
- readable surrounding landscape;
- settlement scale legible before individual building detail;
- no cinematic fantasy exaggeration;
- no dense skyline of monumental stone architecture.

For Waterford, the Suir should be immediately recognizable as the dominant water body, while the southern / southeastern wetland relationship should still be visible.

---

## 10. Candidate 1 QA

Reject the candidate if any of these fail:

1. Suir is not immediately legible.
2. St John's marsh is rendered as a crisp surveyed polygon.
3. Town fills the later medieval city extent.
4. Street pattern becomes a modern orthogonal grid.
5. Houses appear as random rural scatter rather than urban plots.
6. Waterfront becomes a continuous monumental quay.
7. Bakehouse Lane AD1070–1080 alignment is effectively backdated to AD1050.
8. Modern / later Reginald's Tower appears.
9. Dominant stone St Olaf's church appears without a new dating review.
10. Defence and natural water are visually merged.
11. All urban edges have equal confidence and equal density.
12. Image looks more precise than the evidence contract allows.

---

## 11. Evidence anchors used for pre-production

### Arundel Square / Peter Street

Supports 11th-century street surface, three-aisled houses, smaller rear structures, paths and property boundaries.

### 19–21 High Street

Supports High Street as a principal late-Viking street and records an early Viking house at the site.

### Peter Street / Olaf Street

Supports dense late-Viking occupation and also provides a post-1050 chronological control through the c. AD1083 timber date.

### Bakehouse Lane

Supports a substantial late-Viking defence, but dendrochronology centred on AD1070–1080 makes it a negative control for the target year.

### Early Medieval Dwellings and Settlements in Ireland

Supports the generalized Waterford street network and Type 1 / Type 2 plot-house relationship used for visual organization.

### National Monuments Service urban survey

Supports topographic/hydrological context and the rule that the earliest quay must remain unresolved rather than mapped as a precise line.
